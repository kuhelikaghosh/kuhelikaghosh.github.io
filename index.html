About

Water, energy, and the policies that connect them

I am an applied economist working on water and energy policy, and a Ph.D. candidate in Agricultural & Applied Economics at the University of Georgia. My research examines how farmers, households, and utilities respond to resource scarcity and policy — drawing on both field-intensive and data-driven methods, including discrete choice experiments and other stated-preference approaches to non-market valuation.

Before beginning my doctoral studies, I trained and worked as a rural development professional and policy researcher — as a Pre-Doctoral Fellow with the IWMI–Tata Water Policy Program and as a research consultant with the International Water Management Institute in India. That work spanned irrigation management by public and private institutions, the water–energy–climate nexus, agricultural insurance, and sustainable farming practices, and it continues to shape the questions I ask today.

Job Market Paper

Groundwater Irrigation Expansion and Its Impact on Utility Peak Load

Utility-year panel, 2000–2024 · Draft available on request

Irrigation is a widely recognized adaptation to the agricultural productivity losses caused by climate change, and farmers across the United States are increasingly adopting groundwater irrigation as temperatures rise. Because these irrigation pumps are predominantly electric, this adaptation is also a growing source of electricity demand — yet the role of groundwater-based irrigation as a driver of utility peak load has received little quantitative attention, partly because utilities do not report agricultural consumption separately. I exploit exogenous variation in groundwater endowments, set by the geographic distribution of aquifers rather than current climate, to estimate how the number of active groundwater irrigation wells affects utility-level summer peak electricity demand. Using a utility-year panel spanning 2000–2024 that combines EIA operational data with spatial data on irrigation wells, I find that a 1% increase in irrigation wells is associated with a 0.092% increase in summer peak demand (p < 0.001) — about 32 kW of additional peak load per well for the average utility. The results carry implications for grid planning and electricity pricing, and contribute to the literature on adaptation externalities by showing how private groundwater-irrigation decisions can impose costs through the energy system.
